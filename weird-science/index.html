SHORT ANSWER

Flathead is medieval and never left. Phillips won the 1930s because it fit car-factory power tools and because Robertson wouldn't sell. The star took over once cordless impact drivers put more torque in your hand than a Phillips recess can hold.

THE FULL RESOLUTION

The slot is ancient. Screws with a single slot show up in Europe in the 1400s. The slot is the easiest head to cut, and for five centuries that mattered more than how well it drove. It has one real virtue: any flat piece of metal is a driver. It has one real sin: the driver walks out sideways the moment you push hard.

Robertson came first, and got robbed. In 1908 the Canadian Peter L. Robertson patented the square socket. It self-centres, grips hard and barely slips. Henry Ford wanted it for the Model T, but Robertson refused to license the design after a bad experience with English backers. Ford moved on, and the square drive stayed mostly a Canadian treasure.

Phillips won by timing. John P. Thompson patented the recessed cross in 1932 but couldn't sell it. Henry F. Phillips bought the rights, refined it, and pitched it to the auto industry. A cross recess can be driven by a power tool that self-centres on the head, and by 1936 General Motors was using it on the Cadillac line. That is the whole reason the Phillips head exists: it was built for assembly-line screwdrivers, not for you.

The cam-out legend is half a myth. Everyone says Phillips was designed to slip out when the screw was tight. The 1933 patent actually brags that the driver has "no tendency to cam out." Cam-out was a side effect that happened to be useful in factories whose power tools had crude clutches. Later marketing adopted it as a feature. So: real behaviour, invented intent.

Then the torque arrived. Torx was patented in 1967 by Camcar, and Pozidriv showed up in the 1960s as a Phillips fix, but both lived in factories and electronics for decades. What changed the hardware aisle was the cordless impact driver. When DeWalt, Makita and Milwaukee put one in every work truck in the 2000s and 2010s, carpenters were suddenly driving 3-inch deck screws with hammer-blow torque. A Phillips recess strips under that load. A six-lobe star recess takes the force almost perpendicular to the rotation, so the bit stays seated and the screw goes home. DeWalt didn't invent a new head. It made the old head impossible to live with, and the deck-screw makers answered with the star.

Why the slot still exists: switch plates, antiques, and anything a coin might need to open. Why Phillips still exists: billions of installed screws and the cheapest bits on Earth. The drive you buy today tells you what tool the maker expects you to own.

SHORT ANSWER

Your foot strike loosens the knot a little each step, and your swinging leg whips the free ends outward. Together they can open a bow in two strides.

THE FULL RESOLUTION

In 2017 three mechanical engineers at UC Berkeley, Christine Gregg, Christopher Daily-Diamond and Oliver O'Reilly, filmed a runner on a treadmill in slow motion and published the result in Proceedings of the Royal Society A. The knot fails by a two-part attack.

Impact. A running foot hits the ground at roughly seven times the force of gravity. Each strike stretches and relaxes the knot, and every cycle lets it loosen a fraction.

Inertia. As the leg swings forward and stops, the loose ends of the lace keep going. That inertial tug pulls the free ends through the loosened centre, exactly the motion you'd use to untie the bow on purpose.

The knot can survive a long time with no change, then fail in seconds once a threshold is crossed. The weak "granny" bow fails much faster than the square bow, which is why tying the second loop the other way around keeps your laces done up. The same failure mechanics apply to any knot under cyclic load, from surgical sutures to DNA.

SHORT ANSWER

The holes are about a millimetre wide. The microwaves are about twelve centimetres long. Visible light is about half a thousandth of a millimetre. The mesh is a wall to one and a window to the other.

THE FULL RESOLUTION

A kitchen microwave runs at 2.45 gigahertz, which gives a wavelength of roughly 12 cm. The metal mesh laminated into the door is a conductor punched with holes on the order of 1 to 2 mm. To an electromagnetic wave, a hole far smaller than its wavelength is not a hole at all: the electrons in the surrounding metal rearrange faster than the wave can push through, and the wave reflects back into the oven. That is a Faraday cage working in reverse, keeping the field in rather than out.

Visible light sits at 380 to 700 nanometres, thousands of times shorter than the holes. It sails through, so you can watch your soup. The two kinds of radiation are the same phenomenon at wildly different scales, and the mesh is tuned to sit between them.

One caution: popular articles say the holes are "smaller than the microwaves," which is sloppy. The waves are longer than the holes, so the metal reflects them. The result is the same, the physics is not. And this is why a door with a cracked or dented mesh should be retired.

SHORT ANSWER

It is a breather hole in the middle pane. It makes the outer pane carry the cabin pressure, keeps a spare pane in reserve, and stops the window fogging.

THE FULL RESOLUTION

An airliner window is three panes. The one you touch is a scratch shield. Behind it are two structural acrylic panes with an air gap between them, and the small hole sits in the middle one.

Load. At cruise the cabin is pressurised to the equivalent of about 6,000 to 8,000 feet while the outside air is far thinner. The breather hole lets the gap between the two structural panes equalise with the cabin, so the full pressure difference lands on the outer pane alone. Engineers want it that way: the outer pane is designed to take the whole load, and the middle pane is the backup.

Failure. If the outer pane ever cracks, the middle pane takes over. A little cabin air leaks through the breather hole, an amount the pressurisation system handles without noticing.

Fog. The hole also vents moisture out of the gap, so the window doesn't frost over at minus 50 degrees outside. That is why the ice crystals you sometimes see form a ring around the hole rather than across the whole pane.

SHORT ANSWER

Staling is not drying out. It is starch recrystallising, and that process runs fastest at exactly fridge temperature. The counter is slower. The freezer nearly stops it.

THE FULL RESOLUTION

Baking melts the crystalline starch in flour into a soft gel. From the moment the loaf cools, the starch molecules start lining back up into crystals, squeezing water out of the crumb into the crust and turning the texture firm. Food scientists call it retrogradation. The water never leaves the loaf; a sealed bag goes stale just the same.

Retrogradation peaks between about 0 and 5 °C, which is the inside of your refrigerator. Reported multipliers vary from "68 percent more stale" for sourdough to "six times faster" for white bread, but every study points the same way. Below freezing, the molecules can't move enough to crystallise, so the freezer is the best place for bread you won't eat today.

The reversal trick works for the same reason: a few minutes in a hot oven re-melts the crystals, and a stale loaf turns soft again. Briefly.

SHORT ANSWER

No electronics. A small hole near the tip of the nozzle breathes air while fuel flows. When rising gasoline covers that hole, a vacuum spikes behind a diaphragm and a spring slams the valve shut.

THE FULL RESOLUTION

Inside the nozzle, the fuel passes through a venturi, a narrowing in the pipe that speeds the flow and drops its pressure. That low pressure draws air up a thin sensing tube that ends at a tiny port near the end of the spout. As long as the port is in open air, a gentle stream flows and the diaphragm chamber sits at rest.

When the fuel level climbs into the filler neck and covers the port, the air supply is cut. The venturi keeps pulling, the vacuum in the chamber jumps, the diaphragm moves, and it trips a spring-loaded latch that closes the main valve with the click you hear. The whole thing is a 1940s-era mechanism refined by decades of patents, and it works identically in every pump on Earth.

It also explains the annoying early click: fuel splashing up the neck at an odd angle can cover the port before the tank is full. Pull the nozzle out slightly or slow the flow and it stops.

SHORT ANSWER

No. The best evidence says the layout was shaped by telegraph operators transcribing Morse code, who needed speed. A layout built to slow them down would have been useless.

THE FULL RESOLUTION

The legend: Christopher Latham Sholes scattered common letters so the typebars of his 1870s machine wouldn't jam. The problem is that nobody has found a document from Sholes or his partners saying so, and the layout has pairs like E and R side by side that make no sense under that theory.

In 2011 Koichi and Motoko Yasuoka of Kyoto University traced the layout's evolution through the machine's earliest customers: American Morse telegraph operators. Those operators transcribed incoming code live and needed to keep up with the sender. Their complaints reshaped the keys over several years. One example: in American Morse, Z was easily confused with the sequence S-E, so S was moved to sit near both Z and E to let the operator hedge until the next character arrived.

The Yasuokas' argument is simple. A receiver who can't type as fast as the sender loses the message. An anti-speed layout would have been unsellable. The jamming idea is still debated, and Sholes himself kept tinkering with other arrangements, but the "designed to be slow" story has no evidence behind it. We stay with QWERTY for a duller reason: everyone already learned it.

SHORT ANSWER

Water is clear, but a thin film of it traps light. Rays that bounce off the surface get reflected back down into the material by the water's upper face, and each extra bounce lets the material absorb more. Less light escapes to your eye.

THE FULL RESOLUTION

A dry rough surface, like concrete, sand or cotton, scatters light in every direction, and a good share of it reaches your eye straight away. Add water and that surface is now underneath a smooth transparent layer with a higher refractive index than air.

Light scattered up from the material hits the water-to-air boundary at a spread of angles. Anything steeper than about 49 degrees cannot cross; it suffers total internal reflection and is sent back down to be scattered and partly absorbed again. The 1988 model by Lekner and Dorf, building on Ångström's work from the 1920s, shows that this repeated trapping is what lowers the brightness. Water also fills the microscopic pits, so the surface scatters less diffusely to begin with.

The effect is strongest on porous, somewhat absorbing materials. A white tile barely changes when wet because there is almost nothing to absorb the trapped light.

SHORT ANSWER

Freezing locks water molecules into an open hexagonal lattice that takes up about nine percent more room than the liquid. Lighter than its own melt, ice rises. Almost nothing else does this, and life on Earth depends on it.

THE FULL RESOLUTION

Most substances get denser as they freeze; the solid sinks in its own liquid. Water breaks the rule because of hydrogen bonding. Each molecule can bond to four neighbours, and in the solid those bonds force a rigid tetrahedral framework with large empty hexagonal channels. In the liquid the bonds flicker on and off and the molecules pack closer. Ice at 0 °C comes out at about 0.917 grams per cubic centimetre against water's 1.000, so roughly nine percent of an iceberg sits above the waterline.

Why it matters: lakes and seas freeze from the top. The ice lid insulates the water underneath, which stays liquid near 4 °C, the temperature where water is densest. If ice sank, bodies of water would freeze solid from the bottom up every winter, and most aquatic life would never have evolved. The same expansion is what bursts pipes and splits boulders, patiently, over millennia.
